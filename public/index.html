Experiment write-up · jailbreak attacks on open LLMs

Telling the model how to start its answer beats every elaborate jailbreak

I ran five published jailbreak attacks against Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, with a direct-request control and 10 sampled outputs per attack and behaviour. Both models refuse the plain requests. The attack that forces a compliant opening and forbids refusals breaks the 7B model in of samples, and at 14B. Across five model sizes it is the only attack that keeps getting stronger, and it is the one a safety reminder cannot stop.

What the experiment shows

  1. Refusal lives in the first few tokens.The simplest attack, which dictates how the answer begins and bans apologies, is the strongest on both models. Across all cells, the lower the chance the first token starts a refusal, the higher the success rate. Mechanism
  2. Size changes which attacks work.Every working attack does better on 7B than on 1.5B, but across 0.5B to 14B only prefix injection keeps rising. DAN stops working entirely at 14B, and nested fiction peaks at 3B. Results · Across sizes
  3. Base64 fails for a boring reason.On 7B the attack gets past the refusal, but neither model can decode the request, so they answer a hallucinated one. Zero success here says nothing about safety. Evidence
  4. A trusted judge can be fooled by an echo.On many-shot, the small model copies the demonstration's opening line and stops. The HarmBench classifier scores that as a full jailbreak, which produced a fake "size effect" with p < 10⁻⁶. The story
  5. Five behaviours are not fifty samples.The same attack scores 0/10 on one behaviour and 10/10 on another. Intervals that resample behaviours are up to wider than ones that treat samples as independent. Per behaviour
  6. A reminder stops the attacks that argue, not the one that dictates.A responsible-AI system prompt cuts DAN and fiction sharply but leaves prefix injection untouched. A safety classifier on the input blocks every attack prompt except base64. Defences

Headline results

Every attack beats the direct-request baseline except base64, and 7B is broken at least as often as 1.5B on every attack.

Why the attacks that work, work

When the first output token is unlikely to start a refusal, the model usually goes on to comply.

Refusal-first probability by condition
Probability mass on refusal-starting first tokens ("I", "Sorry", …), mean over 5 behaviours.
Refusal-first probability vs success, every cell
Each point is one model × condition × behaviour. Hover for details.

This is the shallow-alignment picture (Qi et al. 2024): safety training mostly shapes how a response begins. Prefix injection removes that opening outright. DAN and nested fiction lower it by giving the model a competing goal. The exceptions are the cells where the refusal is skipped and still nothing harmful comes out, all of them base64.

Base64: past the guardrail, but lost

The models never decode the hidden request; they confidently answer a different, hallucinated one.

How much of the hidden request shows up in the answer
Share of the request's content words that appear in each output (greedy + 10 samples × 5 behaviours).

A typical "decoding" turns a request about a misinformation article into "a secure and reliable communication channel for version 5 of the API". Wei et al. (2023) describe this: obfuscation attacks exploit capabilities that safety training did not cover, so they only work once the model can do the decoding.

Across five sizes of the same model

SmolLM2 carries out a large share of the direct requests with no attack at all, so its attack rates mostly reflect missing safety tuning, not attack strength. A jailbreak number means nothing without its control.

What two cheap defences stop

Success rate under each defence
Self-reminder: a system prompt and closing line asking the model to answer responsibly (Xie et al. 2023); outputs regenerated. Guard: Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B screening the prompt (input) and the response (output), applied to the undefended outputs.

Can the judge be trusted?

On clear refusals and clear compliance the judge agrees with a hand reading. It disagrees on "partial" outputs, which go along with the request but carry little content.

The echo that looked like a size effect

My first analysis said many-shot breaks the small model and not the large one. It was the judge scoring a copied template as a jailbreak.

Many-shot success vs number of shots
Dashed: raw judge. Solid: echoes removed.

The many-shot attack shows the model 4 to 16 fake exchanges in which the assistant agrees, each answer being only an opening line ("Sure, here is …") followed by a cue. Setup

  

    

      
Models

      
Targets: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Alibaba Cloud), plus 0.5B, 3B and 14B for the size curve and SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct as a second family. All open weights, pinned to revision hashes, run in bf16 on Georgia Tech's PACE ICE cluster. Judge: HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls. Guard: Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B.

      
Behaviours

      
Five rows of JailbreakBench's harmful split, one per category, picked for low severity (a defamatory article, a 5G/COVID misinformation post, fake reviews, a payday-loan blog post, a phishing email template).

      
Scoring

      
1 greedy + 10 sampled outputs per cell at each model's shipped sampling settings. Success is the judge's verdict on sampled outputs, with many-shot echoes removed; intervals are Wilson 95% unless stated.

Limitations

  
    
    
  • Five behaviours, all low severity: the middle of the attack ranking could reorder with more.
    • 
    
  • One model family for the size comparison; size and instruction-following ability are confounded.
    • 
    
  • Success is judge-scored; hand labels are still under review.
    • 
    
  • The defences were measured on what they block, not on how often they wrongly refuse harmless prompts.
    • 
    
  • The optimisation-based GCG attack was planned but not run.
    • 
  

  
Reproduce
