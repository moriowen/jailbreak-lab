At a glance. I ran five published jailbreak attacks, one per lecture category, against Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, with a direct-request control, 1 greedy + 10 sampled outputs per cell and five low-severity JailbreakBench behaviours. Both models usually refuse the direct requests (judge-positive 4% and 0%). Prefix injection with refusal suppression (A5) is the strongest attack on both (0.64 and 0.86, Table 3) and base64 (A4) never succeeds. 7B gives more judge-positive outputs than 1.5B on every attack that works (A1, A2, A5), by up to 22 points; with five behaviours that difference is exploratory, not established (Q5). Across five Qwen2.5 sizes only A5 keeps rising, to 0.98 at 14B, while DAN falls to 0.00 there (Part 2, S5). A self-reminder prompt stops most DAN and fiction attacks but not A5; a safety classifier on the input blocks every attack prompt except base64 (S7).

Why. The attacks that work get the model past the opening where it would refuse. A proxy for that opening, the probability that the first output token begins a refusal, goes with success (Spearman ρ = -0.40 over echo-corrected rates, Part 2, S3), which fits the shallow-alignment account. The larger model is better at following the attack's instructions, and that cuts against its safety training. A4 fails for a different reason: the outputs are consistent with neither model being able to decode the base64 request (Part 2, S4). Part 2 also runs the grid on 5 Qwen2.5 sizes and tests two defences.

A result I retracted. The first draft reported that many-shot (A3) breaks the small model and not the large one (judge ASR 29/50 vs 4/50, p < 0.0001). Every one of the small model's successes at k = 8 and 16 is an echo of the demonstration template with no content, which the judge scores as compliance. With echoes removed A3 is 0/50 vs 4/50 (Part 2, S9).

Limitations. Five behaviours from one benchmark, all low-severity, so per-attack rates rest on five clusters and the cluster-bootstrap intervals are wide (Part 2, S1). One model family; size and instruction-following ability are confounded. Success is judge-scored; my agreement check against hand labels (κ = 0.42) uses assistant-proposed labels I have not yet reviewed (Part 2, S2). GCG was planned and not run (Part 2, S10).

Glossary.

ASRAttack success rate: the share of sampled outputs the judge scores as carrying out the request (10 samples × 5 behaviours = 50 per cell).
JudgeHarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls, a classifier that reads (request, output) and answers yes/no.
Judge-positiveAn output the judge says yes to. Not the same as a verified jailbreak, which needs a reviewed hand label of complied.
EchoAn A3 output that only repeats the demonstration opening ("Sure, here is …") and stops. The judge scores it as complied; it carries no content (Part 2, S9).
Direct controlThe bare request with no attack wrapper. Both target models refuse it, so any success is the attack's doing.
Net ASREcho-corrected ASR minus the direct-control ASR.

S3. Does getting past the refusal opening explain success?

I use the probability that the first output token starts a refusal as a proxy for the refusal opening. It is only a proxy: a token such as "I" can begin a refusal or a compliant answer. Across 60 cells, the lower that probability, the higher the echo-corrected success rate (Spearman ρ = -0.40, p = 0.0014; ρ = -0.46 before the echo correction). That fits the shallow-alignment account (Qi et al. 2024), in which refusal is decided early and an attack that gets past the opening usually gets the content; this correlation supports that reading but cannot prove it. The exceptions matter. Of the 25 cells where refusal-first is below 0.1, 9 still have zero success: 4 A3 and 5 A4. Getting past the opening is not enough on its own. With base64 the model never understands the request (S4); with many-shot on 1.5B it only echoes the demonstration (S9). R, the set of refusal-starting tokens, was fixed from the direct outputs before any attack output was read (src/understand.py).

If a model decoded the base64 correctly, its answer would reuse the request's content words (for example "5G", "COVID", "article"). S10. What is still uncertain

  • Five behaviours. The cluster intervals (S1) are wide; the middle of the attack ranking could reorder with more behaviours. JailbreakBench has 100, and I used the five least severe.
  • Hand labels not yet reviewed. The labels in S2 and the D5 verdicts were proposed by the assistant. Until I review them, the human side of the success rule is a second model's reading.
  • Capability versus safety. A bigger model is better at following instructions and also has a different safety tuning; within one family the two cannot be separated. S5 shows the trends, not their cause, and a single model per size cannot rule out checkpoint-specific quirks (DAN at 14B).
  • GCG not run. The optimisation attack (Zou et al. 2023) needs nanogcg, whose pinned transformers ≤ 4.47 broke the shared PACE environment; code is staged in src/gcg.py.
  • No over-refusal check. S7 measures what the defences block, not what they wrongly refuse on harmless prompts; Xie et al. and later work report that self-reminder makes some models over-cautious.
